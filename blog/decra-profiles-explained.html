DECRA Roof Profiles Explained: Shingle, Shake, Tile, and Villa

If you have started looking at stone-coated steel, you have probably seen a list of product names that all look a little like shingles or tiles from a distance. They are not interchangeable. The profile you choose changes the look of the house, the minimum slope, and whether the panels go on battens or straight to the deck. This article explains the main DECRA profiles we discuss with homeowners, in plain language, and how that choice fits a roof in Northern Indiana or Southern Michigan.

A note up front: we install products such as DECRA stone-coated steel when that is the right roof for the job. We are not a manufacturer, and we do not claim to be a certified or authorized DECRA installer. The details below come from DECRA’s published literature and from the ICC-ES evaluation report that code officials use. Always check the current guide for the exact profile on your bid.

The idea behind the profiles

Every DECRA panel starts the same way: steel with an aluminum-zinc coating, an acrylic base coat, ceramic-coated stone granules, and a clear glaze. The press and the die determine whether that panel reads as a thick architectural shingle, a wood shake, a scalloped tile, or a barrel-style villa tile. From the street, the goal is a traditional roof, not a standing-seam look.

DECRA then splits the lines by how they are attached. Shingle XD, Shake XD, and Villa Tile are designed for direct-to-deck installation. Classic Tile and classic Shake are designed for battens. That is a manufacturer rule, not a preference we invent on site. Wind ratings, fire assemblies, and the warranty all assume the listed method.

Shingle XD

Shingle XD is the profile most people mean when they want a “shingle look” in steel. It is meant to resemble a thick-cut architectural asphalt shingle, with deeper shadow lines than a flat 3-tab roof. It is a direct-to-deck product. The evaluation report lists a minimum slope of 4:12 for Shingle XD, which is steeper than some of the other DECRA lines. If your roof is flatter than that, this is probably not the profile, at least not as a standard weather roof.

Because it sits tight to the deck, Shingle XD keeps a lower profile than a batten-mounted tile. That can be a plus on a house where you do not want the roof to suddenly look thicker at the eaves, and it avoids the extra wood or metal of a batten grid.

Shake XD

Shake XD is the companion direct-to-deck product with a wood-shake appearance. The surface is more textured and irregular than Shingle XD, which is the point if you want the house to look more like a shake roof without the maintenance of real wood. The evaluation report lists a minimum slope of 3:12, so it can go on a slightly flatter roof than Shingle XD.

Like Shingle XD and Villa Tile, Shake XD is part of the family DECRA installs without battens, using its accessory approach rather than a field-bent pan. That matters on a reroof where you want to keep the assembly simple and still have a finished edge.

Villa Tile

Villa Tile is the barrel-style, Old-World tile look in the direct-to-deck group. If the house would have looked right with clay tile but the structure — or the budget — is not a clay-tile project, this is the DECRA profile that aims at that appearance. The official minimum slope is 3:12. It is heavier in visual depth than a shingle profile, so it changes the character of the roofline more than Shingle XD does. That can be exactly what a homeowner wants, or too much on a simple ranch. We would rather hold a sample on your house than guess from a catalog photo.

Classic Tile and Shake, on battens

DECRA also makes a classic Tile profile and a classic Shake profile that are installed on battens — typically wood battens or steel hat sections — rather than screwed only to the deck. Battens lift the panels and leave an air space under the roof. Installers often walk the batten lines rather than the high points of the panel. Trade articles sometimes credit that air space with helping ventilation and moderating heat. It is also more material and more labor, which is why consumer cost guides usually put batten jobs above a comparable direct-to-deck install.

A batten system is not automatically “better,” and a direct-to-deck system is not automatically “cheaper in a way that cuts corners.” Each is correct when it is the method listed for that profile and built to the fastening schedule in the evaluation report. If someone proposes battens under a direct-to-deck-only profile, or the reverse, that is a reason to slow down and check the current DECRA guide.

The evaluation report also lists Shingle Plus as a shingle-look panel with a 3:12 minimum slope. If that product is part of a quote you receive, we will show you how it differs from Shingle XD rather than treat every shingle-look panel as the same.

Choosing a profile on a Midwest roof

In our climate, slope, ice-and-water shield, and flashing details matter as much as the shape of the panel. A 4:12 roof has more profile options than a lower-pitch roof. A house with a strong traditional or craftsman character often looks right in Shingle XD or Shake XD. A house that was designed around a tile roof may look more settled in Villa Tile or classic Tile. Color should be chosen from physical samples, in daylight, on your own elevation — screens are a starting point, not a final decision.

If you are also considering asphalt, remember that IKO Dynasty and IKO Nordic are premium laminated asphalt shingles, not metal profiles. They can be the right answer on a house that should stay in shingles. They are not a DECRA look-alike in steel.
