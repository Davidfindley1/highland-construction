What Is Stone-Coated Steel Roofing — and Is It Worth It in Michigan and Indiana?

If you are thinking about a new roof in Northern Indiana or Southern Michigan, you will hear a lot of product names. Some of them sound similar and are not the same thing. Stone-coated steel roofing is one option we are asked about often, and it is the specialty we emphasize at Highland Construction. This article explains what it actually is, how it differs from asphalt and standing seam metal, and how to judge whether the higher upfront cost is worth it on a Midwest home.

What stone-coated steel actually is

Stone-coated steel is a metal roof that is made to look like a traditional shingle, shake, or tile roof. The panel starts as structural sheet steel with an aluminum-zinc alloy coating — the same family of coating often sold under names such as Galvalume. That coating is there to help the steel resist rust. Over that, manufacturers apply a primer, an acrylic base coat, a layer of ceramic-coated stone granules, and a clear acrylic glaze.

Those granules are the part you see from the street. They give the roof its color and texture, they help protect the coating from sunlight, and they are why this product does not have the smooth, ribbed look of a standing-seam metal roof. Independent evaluation of DECRA panels, a widely used brand in this category, describes the steel as ASTM A792 sheet with an AZ50 aluminum-zinc coating, about 0.017 inches thick, weighing about 1.3 pounds per square foot installed — roughly 130 pounds per roofing square.

Common profiles include a thick architectural-shingle look, a wood-shake look, and tile looks from a scalloped tile to a barrel-style “villa” tile. DECRA names several of these Shingle XD, Shake XD, Villa Tile, Tile, and Shake. Some fasten directly to the deck; others go over battens. The right method is the one listed for that profile, because wind, fire, and warranty coverage follow the tested assembly.

How it is not asphalt, and not standing seam

Asphalt shingles are a different product. They are built around a fiberglass mat, coated with asphalt, and finished with granules. That includes premium laminated shingles such as IKO Dynasty and IKO Nordic. Those names come up alongside DECRA because IKO also owns the DECRA metal line. The connection is easy to misread. Dynasty and Nordic are high-end laminated asphalt shingles, not stone-coated metal. Nordic uses polymer-modified asphalt and carries a Class 4 impact rating; Dynasty uses traditional asphalt and a Class 3 rating. Both are solid premium shingle options when metal is not the right fit.

Standing-seam metal is also different. It is usually a painted or bare metal panel with raised vertical seams and a smooth face. It is excellent on many roofs, especially lower slopes, and it has a distinct look. Stone-coated steel is chosen when someone wants the durability of steel with the appearance of a shingle, shake, or tile roof rather than a contemporary metal roof.

How it tends to perform

DECRA describes a Lifetime Limited Warranty on single-family homes in the U.S. and Canada, covering manufacturing defects that cause leaks, panel blow-off in winds up to 120 mph, and penetration by hailstones up to 2.5 inches. That is the manufacturer’s warranty, with limits, exclusions, and later-year proration. It is not a Highland Construction warranty, and we do not claim to be a certified or authorized factory installer.

DECRA also reports that its panel styles meet UL 2218 Class 4 impact resistance, the highest class in that steel-ball test. Class 4 is useful information, especially in a region that does see hail, but no roof should be called hail-proof. An indent or a bit of granule loss can still be an appearance issue even when the panel has not been punctured.

Fire ratings are often summarized too quickly as “Class A.” The official evaluation report for DECRA panels allows Class A, Class B, or Class C, depending on the underlayment and, in some assemblies, a barrier board. The steel itself does not burn, but the rating that matters is the installed roof assembly. That is a detail we sort out on the job, not a slogan.

Two other homeowner questions come up all the time: noise and walking on the roof. The stone coating and a normal roof deck, underlayment, and attic insulation usually keep rain noise in the same range as asphalt. A house with little or no attic may still benefit from a heavier underlayment. You can walk on these roofs, but not anywhere you like. Soft-soled shoes, stay off the side laps, and step where the manufacturer shows a walking zone for that profile.

Is it a good fit for Midwest winters?

Northern Indiana and Southern Michigan bring freeze-thaw cycles, snow, ice at the eaves, and the occasional hailstorm. Stone-coated steel handles wet-and-cold weather well when it is flashed correctly and when ice-and-water shield is used where cold-climate practice calls for it. DECRA’s own installation guidance says to use ice-and-water shield in cold climates.

What it will not do by itself is stop ice dams. Ice dams form when heat escaping into the attic melts snow on the upper roof and that water refreezes at the cold eaves. The real prevention work is air sealing, insulation, and keeping the roof deck cold. A metal surface can shed snow more readily than rough asphalt, but a granulated coating is not as slick as standing seam. If the attic is leaky, ice can still form at the edge. We would rather say that plainly than oversell the material.

What it costs, and when it can be worth it

National cost guides put stone-coated steel in a higher bracket than asphalt and in a similar range to other metal roofs. HomeGuide’s published ranges, which we treat as national averages rather than a local bid, are about $7 to $13 per square foot installed for stone-coated steel, $4 to $6 for architectural asphalt shingles, $3 to $5 for more basic asphalt, and $9 to $16 for standing seam. HomeGuide also lists a typical service life of 40 to 70 years for stone-coated steel, compared with about 15 to 20 years for 3-tab shingles and 20 to 30 years for architectural shingles.

Those numbers move with roof size, pitch, stories, valleys, tear-off, and deck repairs. A steep, cut-up roof will not land on the low end of a national average, which is why we do not post a unit price. If you want a number for your house, ask us for a free estimate.

Whether it is “worth it” depends less on a slogan and more on how long you plan to stay, how tired the current roof is, whether you want a shingle look without another asphalt cycle in 20 years, and whether hail or insurance conversations matter on your street. For some homes, a premium asphalt shingle such as Dynasty or, where it is sold, Nordic is the more practical choice. For others, stone-coated steel is the better long-term roof. We will help you compare those options honestly.
