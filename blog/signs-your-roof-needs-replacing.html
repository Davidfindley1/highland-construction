Signs Your Roof Needs Replacing Before Winter

The best time to find out that a roof is tired is not the first night it ices over. In Northern Indiana and Southern Michigan, late fall is when a small leak becomes a stained ceiling, and when a missing shingle becomes a wet deck. This article is a practical look at the signs that a roof may need repair or replacement before winter, and what to do with that information. It is written for homeowners, not as a scare list, and it does not assume every aging roof has to come off tomorrow.

Start with age, but do not stop there

A typical 3-tab asphalt roof is often in the 15- to 20-year range of useful life. Architectural shingles more often land around 20 to 30 years, and some premium laminated shingles are marketed for a longer service life. Those are averages from published cost and lifespan guides, not a countdown clock on your house. A roof that was poorly ventilated, or installed over a damp deck, can be done early. A roof that was well built and lightly weathered can look serviceable past the average.

If you know the year the roof was put on, write it down and use it as context. If you do not, the shingles themselves usually tell a clearer story than a guess.

What you can see from the ground

Walk the property on a bright day and look at the slopes you can see safely from the yard or a window. You do not need to get on the roof to notice several important things:

  • Shingles that are curling, cupping, or looking unusually ragged at the edges.
  • Cracked, broken, or missing shingles, especially on hips, ridges, and the edges of the roof.
  • Bare or dark patches where the granules have worn off and the asphalt mat is showing.
  • A lot of loose granules in the gutters or at downspout splash blocks. A little granule loss is normal on a newer roof after a hard rain; a thick, sandy accumulation on an older roof is a warning.
  • Shingles that look like they have slipped, or a ridge that no longer sits straight.
  • Flashing at the chimney, skylights, or wall intersections that is rusty, pulled, or caulked over and over again.
  • Sagging along a slope or a dip between rafters, which can point to a deck or structural problem, not just old shingles.

From inside, look at the top-floor ceilings and the attic, if you can do so safely. Recurring stains after rain or snowmelt, rusty nail tips, damp sheathing, or daylight visible through the deck are all reasons to have the roof checked. A single old stain that has been dry for years may be a repaired leak. A stain that darkens every time the weather turns is not something to wait out until spring.

Ice-dam clues from last winter

If you had ice ridges at the eaves last year, or water that showed up at exterior walls after a thaw, the roof covering may only be part of the story. Ice dams form when heat escaping into the attic melts snow, and the water refreezes at the cold edge. A worn shingle roof gives that water more ways inside, but a brand-new roof will not fix a leaky, under-insulated attic by itself. When we look at a house with a history of ice dams, we talk about the roof and about air sealing and insulation. Skipping that conversation is how people end up with a new roof and the same stain.

Repair, replace, or wait until spring

Not every problem is a full reroof. A handful of blown shingles after a wind event, a failed pipe boot, or a short run of tired flashing can often be repaired and monitored. If the shingles are widely brittle, the granules are largely gone, there are already two layers on the deck, or leaks are showing in more than one place, replacement is usually the cleaner path. Adding another layer of asphalt over a worn roof can hide deck damage and is limited by code in any case.

Waiting until spring is sometimes reasonable for a cosmetic issue on a roof that is still shedding water. It is a poor plan if you already have active leaks, soft decking, or a forecast for heavy snow on a roof you do not trust. Mid-winter replacements are possible, but they are harder on everyone, and cold weather is a rough time to be opening a house to the sky. Looking at the roof in November gives you options that January does not.

If replacement is in front of you

You do not have to make the material decision the same day you get the news. For many homes in our area, the choice is between a quality architectural or premium asphalt shingle — including products such as IKO Dynasty, or IKO Nordic where it is sold — and a longer-life stone-coated metal roof. Dynasty and Nordic are laminated asphalt shingles, not metal. Stone-coated steel, including products such as DECRA, is a different system with a higher typical upfront cost and a longer typical service life. We will explain both honestly, without pretending one is the only responsible choice.

What we will not do is invent a job count, a factory certification, or a price we have not tied to your roof. A free estimate is the right next step: we look at the slopes, the flashing, the attic if we can, and the condition of the deck as far as it can be judged, then we put a clear recommendation in writing.
