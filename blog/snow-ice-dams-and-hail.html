How Stone-Coated Metal Roofs Handle Snow, Ice Dams, and Hail

Winter is when a roof in Northern Indiana or Southern Michigan has to earn its keep. Snow sits on the slope. Ice builds at the eaves. A late-spring hailstorm can undo a roof that looked fine in October. Stone-coated steel is often sold as a winter roof, and some of that reputation is deserved. Some of it needs a clearer explanation. This article covers what the material actually does with snow, ice dams, and hail — and what still depends on the house underneath.

Snow on a granulated metal roof

A smooth standing-seam roof can shed snow in sheets once the surface warms or the slope is steep enough. That is one reason people like standing seam in snow country, and also why snow guards are sometimes added so the slide does not dump onto a walkway or a lower roof.

Stone-coated steel behaves a little differently. The ceramic-coated granules that give the panel its color also give it texture. The roof is still metal, and snow will move on it, but it is not as slick as a bare or painted standing-seam panel. In practice, snow may linger longer than it would on standing seam and still clear more readily than it often does on a rough, aging asphalt roof. The slope, sun, and how the attic is insulated matter at least as much as the surface.

If a stone-coated roof is in your plans and you have a doorway, sidewalk, or attached lower roof under a steep slope, mention that when we look at the house. Snow management is a design issue, not an afterthought.

Ice dams are mostly an attic problem

Ice dams form when three things are present at once: snow on the roof, heat that melts that snow on the upper slope, and cold at the eaves that refreezes the meltwater into a ridge of ice. Water then ponds behind that ridge and looks for a way into the house, often under shingles or around flashing. Building scientists have been clear about this for a long time. A University of Massachusetts article by Paul Fisette, still widely cited, puts the cause where it belongs: heat lost from the house, not a mysterious failure of the shingles alone. The durable fixes are air sealing the ceiling, enough insulation, and a roof deck that stays cold, with ventilation where the assembly calls for it.

Changing the roof surface does not, by itself, turn off that mechanism. A tight, well-insulated attic can keep ice dams down on asphalt or metal. A leaky, under-insulated attic can build ice on either one. We will not tell you that stone-coated steel “prevents ice dams.” It can be part of a good winter roof, especially when it is detailed correctly, but it is not a substitute for insulation and air sealing.

There is still good reason to use an ice-and-water membrane at the eaves, in valleys, and in other leak-prone areas in our climate. DECRA’s installation guidance for its tile and shake profiles tells installers to use ice-and-water shield as recommended in cold climates. That membrane is backup protection when meltwater is forced up the slope. It is not a reason to ignore the attic.

What hail ratings do and do not mean

Hail is less frequent here than in the central Plains, but it is not rare, and a single storm can bruise a neighborhood of asphalt roofs. Impact resistance is usually discussed in terms of UL 2218, a steel-ball drop test with four classes. Class 4 is the highest: the sample must take two hits in the same spot from a 2-inch steel ball without cracking or opening up.

DECRA states that all of its panel styles — including Shingle XD, Shake XD, Villa Tile, Tile, Shake, and Shingle Plus — meet UL 2218 Class 4. The company also describes warranty coverage related to hailstones up to 2.5 inches that penetrate the roof. An indent or a small amount of granule loss can still be an appearance issue. DECRA’s own hail article notes that this kind of cosmetic mark is not the same as a puncture. No one should call the roof hail-proof.

If you stay with asphalt, the closest comparison is a Class 4 laminated shingle. IKO Nordic is one example: polymer-modified asphalt and a Class 4 rating, aimed at cold, hail-prone markets, and sold only in select areas. IKO Dynasty is a Class 3 laminated shingle. IKO is explicit that those ratings exist in part so homeowners can ask about insurance discounts, and that hail damage is not covered under the IKO limited warranty. That is a meaningful difference from a metal-panel warranty that includes a hail-penetration term. Read both documents, and ask your insurer what, if anything, a Class 3 or Class 4 roof changes in your premium. We cannot promise a discount.

Walking the roof after a storm

After wind or hail, someone usually needs to get on the roof. Stone-coated steel is designed to be walked on, with limits. Soft-soled shoes, stay off the side laps, and step in the zones the manufacturer shows for that profile — often the low “pan” of a tile, or directly over a batten when battens are used. A careless walk can scuff granules or dent a panel. The same caution applies to asphalt, which can crack in the cold.
